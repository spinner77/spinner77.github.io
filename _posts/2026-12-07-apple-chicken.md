Every October I play a game with my apple tree. I call it apple chicken.

The rules are simple. The longer I leave the apples on the tree, the sweeter and juicier they get. But if I leave them one night too long, a hard frost arrives and the whole crop turns to mush. So I stand at the window, check the forecast, check it again, and wait. And wait. And then panic slightly and check it again.

This year the game was as tense as ever, and although I did 'blink first', those apples were absolutely perfect. This is the story of that, and of why waiting has been one of the hardest and most important things I have ever had to learn.

## The tree that taught me timing

My garden has one apple tree, about ten years old. I don't know what variety it is. I remember rescuing it from Aldi, where it was wrapped in plastic and put out for sale with a bunch of other sorry-looking fruit saplings. I planted it, cared for it and waited for my own tasty juicy apples.  When the tree produced its first few apples, I happily picked them and bit into it... Yuck...For years I thought it simply wasn't a very good eating apple. Every time I picked them, they were dry and mealy. I made apple sauce and apple cider vinegar with them so as not to waste the fruit, but neither worked very well.

Last year, for one reason or another, I picked much later than usual. Surprise! The apples were lovely! Sharp, firm and juicy, good enough to eat straight from the tree. The problem had never been the apples. It was my timing.

I left a few on the tree as a test that year, to see how long I could push it. Then one night in October we had our first hard frost. The next morning every apple left on the tree had wrinkled and gone soft. All of them, overnight.

So now I know. The best window is late, but before the first hard frost. Sometime in October... but when exactly? Simple to write down. Much harder to judge when you are standing in front of the tree, here in Buxton, where the frosts can come early and unexpectedly.

## Waiting for a dry day

In early October the weather turned sharply. After a long, dry summer and a mild September, the nights suddenly dropped to 1 or 2°C. My son's weather station recorded 1°C one night, and the next day was stormy, with rain going sideways. Frost felt very close. I decided: Saturday, the whole crop comes in. I was convinced it was the right time.

Then the forecast changed. Saturday would be rain all day, and mild. Temperatures went back up to around 16°C in the daytime and a very balmy 10°C at night. After all that planning, suddenly it was clear, now is not the right time! Apple chicken continued.

It rained. Then it was damp. Then the air was so humid the weather station read 96%. I watched the forecast like it owed me money.

While I waited, I read up on old English folklore. In the old calendar, Michaelmas fell on 10th or 11th October. Tradition says that after Michaelmas you must not pick blackberries, because the Devil, thrown out of heaven, landed in a bramble bush and has spat on them every year since. (The practical reason is that late berries tend to be watery and mouldy.) I had picked the last of my blackberries just in time, and that week, while I waited for my apples, I turned them into cough sweets. I combined the Devil's-spit-free blackberries with whole cloves, cinnamon,  calamondin (an indoor plant I have that has small sour citrus fruits that can be used like lemon), honey and fresh ginger root. The idea was to boil up all the ingredients to a hard crack temperature to make hard boiled sweets. They didn't set properly, and came out more like ginger toffee. I'm choosing to blame the Devil, it definitely wasn't me not using my sugar thermometer because my eldest had borrowed it and not returned it...

## Picking day

On Wednesday 14 October, the pressure rose, the wind dropped and the sun came out. It was dry and settled. Finally, it was time. The day was perfect and I thought I was pushing my luck waiting any longer. Just one frosty night could ruin the whole crop. It turned out I could have waited at least another week. It turned out I could have waited almost a month. The first frost didn’t arrive until 12 November. You win this round of Apple Chicken, apple tree.  Until next year when we battle wit and nerve again!

I picked the lot. I weighed them in my trug on the kitchen scales and proudly looked at **3.17 kg** of apples (about 7 lb). They are yellow with a red flush with streaks and freckles, firm and sharp. A few big ones are for eating this week. The rest, including the marked and misshapen ones, are going into chutney and apple cider vinegar, a second try now I know what ripe actually means.

I pruned the tree the same day. It has grown unchecked for a few years and is now too tall to pick safely. I made sure nothing was wasted:

- **Leaves:** a pound of them, saved for dyeing wool (more about that in my next post).
- **Small twigs:** piled by the compost heap as a bug hotel.
- **Bigger branches:** kept for whittling, and for drying as wood to smoke food with.

And while I was up close, I noticed something. Along the branches, next spring's flower buds are already there. They are fat, slightly fuzzy, and stick out from the stem, while the leaf buds sit flat and slim. In the middle of October, with the leaves turning and the apples coming in, the tree has already quietly prepared for next year.

I keep coming back to that.

## Spoon count

If you haven't come across spoon theory, it's a way of describing limited energy. Each of us starts the day with a handful of spoons, and every task costs some. Some of us have far fewer spoons than others, and some days we have fewer than usual.

As a neurodivergent person, waiting, changing plans and uncertainty can have a bigger effect than just boredom:

- **The waiting days** cost more than you'd think. Checking forecasts, changing plans, holding the decision in my head. That kind of open loop is tiring for my brain. It's easy for me to get distracted,  easy for me to lose focus on the task and before I know it, the frost has come and I've wasted the whole year's growing.
- **Picking and pruning on the same day** was a big spend. Worth it, but I felt it afterwards. It's not the physical exercise,  in fact that is the most beneficial thing for my mind and body. Outdoors, fresh air, purposeful movement all restore my spoons.  No, it's the organising of the products. Tidying up after myself. The practical boring bits are the things that really use up my spoons. I have to make sure the leaves were collected and stored in a way so that they wouldn't rot. The branches needed trimming into usable wood and twigs only fit for making homes for insects.
- **One wet, blustery day**, I didn't make it outside. I stayed in, made the cough sweets that didn't set, planted the leftover ginger root, and spun some wool. Not the day I planned, but still a day.

My point is, I struggle to wait. It goes against my nervous system's need for novelty and action. Changes in plans can sometimes knock me into complete inaction where nothing gets done.

## Unravelling: waiting for the right time

Apple chicken is a funny game now. However, waiting hasn't always been funny.

For a long time, before I knew I was autistic and ADHD, I thought I was simply behind. Everyone my age seemed to be reaching the milestones society expects, moving forward, getting on with life, and I couldn't seem to function as easily as they did. So much of my life felt like it was on hold.

At my worst, with panic attacks and agoraphobia, I would sit and watch the world go by. I mourned the time I was losing and the opportunities I was missing. I grieved for a life I felt I should have had and it all felt deeply unfair and out of my control.

What I couldn't see then was that I didn't have the right tools to move forward yet. And what looked to me like wasted time wasn't wasted at all. I was reading. I was learning. I was crocheting and making things, and using my hands to calm myself down. I was finding ways to regulate a nervous system that was completely overwhelmed. I was cocooning. Resting, learning and understanding, so that one day I could come out changed, and stronger than before.

Like those flower buds in October, something was getting ready, even though nothing seemed to be happening on the outside.

I've come to believe that nobody can know for certain what the right thing to do is at any particular time. The right time is rarely obvious, and so much of it is out of our control. What we can do is trust ourselves, learn from our situation and our surroundings, gather as much information as we can, and then do our best with the tools, knowledge and skills we have at that moment.

This has helped me a lot with regret, blame and shame. If I make a choice and it turns out to be the wrong one, or not the best one, I can give myself some grace. **I did the best I could with what was available to me at the time.** Whatever I learn from it, I can use next time, or share with someone who is in the same place.

Nature keeps reminding me of this. Slow down. Keep your eyes and ears open. The right time will show itself. Stay open to change, and act when it is time to act.
